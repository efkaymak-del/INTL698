What We Are Doing and Why

The skills practiced today are not classroom exercises. They are the exact skills you will use throughout your career:

Writing abstracts that get accepted to conferences (ISA, ECPR, EISA, IPSA).

Peer reviewing colleagues' work with the rigor and constructiveness expected by journals.

Receiving feedback without defensiveness and using it to strengthen your work.

Preparing grant applications that require clear articulation of research design.

By the end of this session, each of you will have a polished abstract ready for submission and a concrete plan for your next professional step.

Conference Abstract Requirements: What Reviewers Want

ISA (Annual Convention)

Title: under 50 words

Abstract: under 200 words

Must include: 3 tag words

Submission types: individual paper, panel (5 papers + chair + discussant), roundtable, innovative panel

Panels not accepted: papers go into general pool

ECPR General Conference

Abstract: 200-300 words

Submit to specific Section or Panel

Evaluated by Section/Panel directors

Strong preference for papers with clear theoretical and methodological framing

Both require: a clear question, stated argument or expected contribution, identified method, and significance. Reviewers spend 2-5 minutes per abstract. Yours must work fast.

The Four-Sentence Abstract Structure

Following Gary King's logic ("Whose mind are you going to change about what?"):

Sentence 1: The Puzzle

What do we not know? What is the gap or debate? "Despite extensive research on X, we lack an explanation for Y."

Sentence 2: The Argument

What does your paper argue or demonstrate? "This paper argues that..." Never: "This paper explores..." or "This paper examines..."

Sentence 3: The Method

How do you demonstrate this? "Using [method] and [data/cases], I test/trace/analyze..."

Sentence 4: The Contribution

What do we learn? Why does it matter? "The findings challenge/extend/refine..."

Test: Can someone who has never met you summarize your paper in one sentence after reading your abstract? If not, rewrite.

How to Peer Review: The Three-Reading Method

Based on David Carter's peer review framework and standard journal practice:

Reading 1: Overview (2 minutes)

Read the abstract and research design summary quickly. Can you state the question, argument, method, and contribution in your own words? If anything is unclear, note it.

Reading 2: Content Critique (5 minutes)

Is the question answerable? Does the argument follow from the theory? Does the method fit the epistemology and question? Is the case selection justified? Are there logic gaps?

Reading 3: Craft Critique (3 minutes)

Is the language clear and precise? Is the abstract within word limits? Is the writing free of jargon that a generalist would not understand? Does it follow the four-sentence structure?

Your goal is not to be a gatekeeper. Your goal is to help your colleague produce the strongest possible submission.

Giving Constructive Feedback

The principles that make peer review useful rather than demoralizing:

Be Specific

"Your research question is vague" is unhelpful. "Your question asks 'what is the role of identity?' — consider specifying whose identity, in what context, and what outcome you are trying to explain" is useful.

Separate Major from Minor

Major: the argument is unclear, the method does not fit the question, the theory is not named. Minor: word choice, formatting, slight overcount on words.

Offer Alternatives, Not Just Criticism

"Instead of process tracing, have you considered QCA given your medium-N design?" is more helpful than "process tracing seems wrong."

Identify Strengths First

Begin with what works. This is not flattery; it tells the author what to preserve and build on.

Receiving Feedback

This is the harder skill. Some principles:

Do not defend. When someone tells you your abstract is unclear, the correct response is "thank you" and a note, not an explanation of what you meant. If you have to explain it, the writing did not do its job.

Wait 24 hours. Feedback that stings on first reading often becomes the most useful advice after a day of reflection.

Separate the person from the comment. A critique of your abstract is not a critique of your intelligence or your years of work.

Look for patterns. If two reviewers both say your question is unclear, your question is unclear — even if you think it is perfectly clear.

You do not have to accept everything. But you must consider everything seriously and have a reason for any feedback you choose not to incorporate.

Aligning Your Conference Submission with a Grant Application

The work you do for a conference abstract overlaps heavily with what grant applications require. Here is how to leverage each:

What a grant adds beyond a conference abstract:

Budget justification: Why do you need the money? Be specific: "Return flight Famagusta-Brussels EUR 250, 3 nights accommodation EUR 300, conference registration EUR 150."

Feasibility: Can you actually do this? Timeline, access to data, language skills, institutional support.

Expected outcomes: What will the funder get? A conference presentation, a working paper, a journal submission, a dataset.

Strategy

Write your conference abstract first. Then expand it into a grant proposal by adding the literature review, detailed methods section, timeline, and budget. The core — question, argument, method, significance — remains the same.

Apply for ECPR travel grants and other funding at the same time as or shortly after submitting your conference abstract.

Activity Block 1: Abstract Revision / Drafting

You have 20 minutes.

If you submitted in advance: revise your abstract using the four-sentence structure. Does it pass the test on Slide 5?

If you did not submit: draft your abstract now. Open the companion app on your phone or laptop and use the Abstract Builder.

Checklist before peer review:

1. Is there a clear, specific question (not just a topic)?

2. Is there an explicit argument (not "explores" or "examines")?

3. Is the method named and specific?

4. Is the contribution stated (what changes in our understanding)?

5. Is it under 200 words (ISA) or 300 words (ECPR)?

Activity Block 2: Peer Review Round

You have 40 minutes. Each person reviews 2 abstracts.

Instructions:

1. You will receive two abstracts from your colleagues (distributed by the instructor).

2. Use the peer review rubric (Slide 9 or the companion app) to evaluate each abstract.

3. For each abstract, write: (a) one paragraph of overall assessment, (b) 2-3 major suggestions, (c) 1-2 minor suggestions.

4. Be constructive. Your feedback should help your colleague submit a stronger abstract.

5. Return your reviews to the author and to the instructor.

Remember: you are practicing for the role of journal reviewer and conference panel discussant. These skills will define your professional reputation.

Activity Block 3: Research Design Presentations

5 minutes per student + 5 minutes feedback.

Your 5-minute presentation must cover:

1. Research question (one sentence)

2. Why it matters (the gap in the literature, the puzzle)

3. Theoretical framework (what theory, why this one)

4. Method and cases (how you will answer the question)

5. Expected contribution (what changes if you are right)

Feedback structure (from the group):

Each audience member offers one specific suggestion. The instructor provides integrative feedback focusing on epistemological alignment, case selection logic, and feasibility.

Activity Block 4: Conference + Grant Strategy

Open discussion. Each student identifies:

1. Target conference: Which conference, which year, which section/panel?

2. Submission deadline: When must the abstract be submitted?

3. Funding source: Which grant will you apply for? (ECPR? TUBITAK? Departmental?)

4. Funding deadline: When must the grant application be submitted?

5. Paper deadline: When must the full paper be ready?

Use the companion app's Funding Tracker and Conference Selector to fill in specifics. Record your plan in the PhD Timeline Planner.

Activity Block 5: Revision Time

20 minutes to revise using the feedback you received.

Incorporate your peer reviewers' suggestions. Revise your abstract. Update your research design summary if needed.

By the end of this block you should have:

1. A revised abstract ready for submission (or very close)

2. A clear target conference and deadline

3. An identified funding source with deadline

4. A list of revisions still needed for your full paper
